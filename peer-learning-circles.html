Peer learning circles: how to run one

A peer learning circle is a small standing group of practitioners who teach each other from the work in front of them, and who write down what should travel to everyone else.

By Jonathan Davis · Published November 16, 2026 · 13 min read

A course moves knowledge outward from a few people. A circle moves it sideways, from practitioner to practitioner: the handoff that actually fails, the prompt that kept an agent honest, the customer question your process cannot answer. If those lessons stay in private chats, the organization pays to learn them again.

This guide shows how to run a circle that people will keep attending. It is the practical form of a habit in the learning organization guide: peers teach peers, in a format small enough to repeat. It also fits the idea of scaled learning, because the point is not the meeting. The point is that what the circle learns becomes available to people who were never in it.

What a circle is, and what it is not

A circle is a standing group of about five to eight people who share a craft or a problem, meet on a calendar, and work from live challenges rather than from a syllabus. Someone facilitates. The group leaves each time with a commitment and, when the conversation produced one, a lesson worth filing.

It is not a status meeting, and it is not a class with an expert and a deck. A member may teach a method for ten minutes when the group asked. Past eight people, the hour becomes a performance. Below four, you lose the range that makes peers more useful than a single colleague.

People join for a season, bring a real situation, and miss a meeting only when they must. Guests come when the group invites them for a reason.

Rhythm, roles and the first meeting

Meet often enough that the work between sessions is still fresh. Monthly is a solid default for a cross-team circle. A team in the middle of a hard change may want every two weeks for a while. An hour is enough if the agenda is tight. Ninety minutes is the upper end before it becomes a workshop.

Name three roles, which can rotate.

  • Facilitator. Protects the agenda, the airtime and the close. Does not need to be the most experienced person in the craft. Often should not be.
  • Members. Bring a situation, listen to understand, offer what they have actually seen, and leave with something they will try.
  • Scribe. Catches the lesson and the commitments in the group's own words. This can be the facilitator in a small circle, as long as the notes are visible.

Use the first meeting to agree the purpose, the season, where notes live, and a plain confidentiality rule: stories stay in the room unless the person who owns the story agrees to share the lesson, with identifying detail removed.

We run mentoring circles on this pattern, with a trained facilitator and a path into the member knowledge library. You can also start a circle inside your own company with the agenda below. The monthly role-based cohorts are a close cousin: people in the same role, a standing hour, live work.

An agenda that fits in an hour

  1. Arrive and name the work. Five minutes. Each person says, in a sentence, what they are carrying today. No speeches.
  2. Check last time's commitments. Five to ten minutes. What did you try, and what happened? This is the part circles skip, and it is the part that makes the circle matter. A commitment that is never revisited teaches people to promise lightly.
  3. One or two live situations. The heart of the hour. A member spends a few minutes on the situation, without being helped too early. The group asks questions until it understands. Only then does it offer what its members have seen, tried or would try. Advice that arrives before the question is understood is usually about the adviser's week, not the member's problem.
  4. Commit. The person with the situation says what they will do before the next meeting. One action. The group witnesses it.
  5. Capture. If the hour produced a lesson that would help someone who was not here, the scribe reads a four-line draft: situation, what we tried or saw, what we recommend, when it applies. The group corrects it. The owner of the story agrees it can be shared.

If more than two people need the floor, decide at the start whose situations will be heard today and put the others first next time. A circle that tries to serve everyone equally serves no one in particular.

How to facilitate without taking over

The facilitator protects attention. A few moves cover most hours.

  • Interrupt a monologue kindly and return to the person whose situation is on the table.
  • Separate questions from advice. Say which phase the group is in. People will mix them unless you mark the line.
  • Ask “what have you seen?” more often than “what should they do?” Peer learning runs on experience, not on opinions delivered with confidence.
  • Notice who has not spoken. Ask them about the part of the situation they are closest to. Do not force a performance.
  • Protect the close. An hour of rich talk with no commitment and no note will feel good and change little.
  • Watch for a story that is really a request for permission. Name it. The circle can think with someone. It cannot secretly become their manager.

The ScaledLearning Certified Learning Facilitator path trains people to run circles, learning retrospectives and workshops. You do not need the certificate to start. You do need someone holding the process so the most senior member does not hold it by default.

Capture without killing the conversation

The capture step fails in two directions. Too little, and the circle is a private good. Too much, and people perform for the notes. Keep the unit small. The four-line lesson from the retrospective guide is the right size here too. Write it in the last five minutes, not as homework that nobody does.

Agree a home for the notes before the second meeting: a place other people already look, with an owner and a review date. An agent can tidy the wording. The circle confirms the substance. Stale notes are worse than none once an agent will repeat them. See knowledge management with AI agents.

Keep commitments in the group's ordinary task list, separate from the lesson. The lesson is for other people. The commitment is for the member who made it.

Keep the circle alive

Circles die when nobody brings a real problem, when the agenda dissolves into updates, or when one person facilitates and chases commitments until they stop. Rotate the facilitator. Start each hour with last time's commitments. Once a season, ask whether the circle is still about the right craft and which lessons were actually reused. If none were reused, change the capture habit.

Pair the circle with other formats instead of asking it to do everything. A facilitated workshop is for a bounded question that needs a product by the end of the day. A retrospective is for a team looking at a cycle it just lived. A circle is for practitioners across teams, over a season, teaching each other from the work. Our courses in designing in-the-workflow learning and capturing tacit knowledge are the longer versions of those two skills.

For the ideas underneath the format, read Context-Driven on the shared context that makes both people and agents useful, Continuous Conversations on replacing the big occasional event with a rhythm, and The Perfect AI Organization on designing a place where people and agents learn in public. If you want a circle run with you rather than a blank page, start with mentoring circles or a role-based cohort.
