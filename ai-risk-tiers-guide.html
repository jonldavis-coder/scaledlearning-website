AI risk tiers: how to sort a use before you scale it

Risk tiers tell a team how much review an AI use needs before it runs, and how much human oversight stays in the loop after it does. Three tiers are enough to start.

By Jonathan Davis · Published November 16, 2026 · 13 min read

One habit sends every model and agent through a committee, so a drafting aid waits for weeks. The other treats every use as a toy, so an agent that writes into customer accounts goes live because a pilot felt promising. Risk tiers are how you stop doing both.

A tier is the review and oversight you match to what can go wrong. This model uses three tiers. It expands the risk-tier block in the AI and data governance guide. It is not a legal classification. Stricter duties from your industry, contracts or locations raise the tier. They do not lower it.

Why one rule for every use fails

A policy strict enough for an agent that acts on customers is too heavy for someone outlining a workshop, so people route around it. A policy light enough for outlining is too light for the agent. Tiers let you say yes quickly where harm is limited and reversible, and slowly where it is not. The question is what happens if the output is wrong, biased, leaked or acted on, and how hard that is to undo.

List the uses you already have, including the informal ones: owner, data touched, and whether a person checks the output before it matters. Tiers applied to an invisible estate are a poster.

Three tiers

Name them in language a practitioner will actually say out loud. We use low, medium and high. Write one short paragraph for each that your teams can apply without a specialist in the room.

  • Low. A draft, summary, list of options or piece of scaffolding that stays inside the team until a person reviews it. It does not act on a customer, an account, a payment or a system of record. A wrong answer is easy to see and cheap to discard.
  • Medium. The output informs a decision, leaves the team as something a person will send, or an agent answers colleagues from internal knowledge. Harm is real, and a person still stands between the model and the consequence.
  • High. The system acts, or a mistake is hard to undo: it changes an account, moves money, denies or grants something a person cares about, handles sensitive personal data, or a quiet error can spread before anyone looks. Design the approval point so a tired reviewer can still say no.

When a use could sit in two tiers, place it in the higher one until the design changes. A human check lowers the tier only when the person sees what they are accepting, has the context to judge it, and can say no in practice.

How to place a use

Walk every new use through the same questions and write the answers. A verbal “it is probably fine” is how a medium use becomes a high one unnoticed.

  1. Purpose. What decision or work is this for? If the purpose is fuzzy, you cannot judge the harm.
  2. Who is affected if the output is wrong, and do they have a way to notice?
  3. What data it reads and writes, including documents, lessons and prompts that contain customer or employee detail. Data you would not paste into an email does not become safe because a model is holding it.
  4. What a bad outcome looks like, in concrete terms: a wrong customer message, a leaked document, a bad decision, a silent change to a record.
  5. How reversible that outcome is, and how quickly someone would know.
  6. Who can switch it off on a bad day, and whether that person is named.

The owner can place a low use against the written paragraph. A medium use gets a short review by someone other than the owner. A high use gets a deeper review, a named sponsor, and an explicit design for oversight, logging and pause. Read LLMs for Leaders before arguing a tier on a use that asks a model to know something it cannot know.

What each tier requires of you

Write the requirements as things a team can show, not as principles they can admire.

  • Low. A named owner. A one-line purpose. A habit that a person looks before the output leaves the team. No connection that lets the tool act on systems of record. If the tool retains prompts, know that and keep sensitive material out.
  • Medium. Everything in low, plus a short written review, a data note (what it may read, what it must not), logging enough to reconstruct a bad answer, and a place to record surprises. If the agent retrieves lessons or documents, those sources need owners and review dates, as described in knowledge management with AI agents. The quality of the library is part of the risk.
  • High. Everything in medium, plus a sponsor, explicit points where a person approves, a tested way to pause the system, a record someone can read after the fact, and an incident path: who is told, who is helped, what is learned. Oversight that exists only in a policy document does not count. It has to be visible in the way the agent is built.

A low use that waits a month for a committee is a failure of the system. A high use that ships because a demo went well is the same failure in the other direction.

Move the tier when the use changes

The tier belongs to the use as it actually runs, not to the label it had at kickoff. Re-place it when any of these change: the data it can see, whether it can act or only draft, who relies on it, how reversible a mistake is, or how widely it has spread. A drafting aid that someone later connects to the customer inbox has changed tier, even if the project name stayed the same.

The owner confirms the tier on a schedule, and sooner if the design changes. An agent can remind them. It cannot pick the new tier. If a use is running above its tier, add the missing controls or narrow the use until it fits.

Keep a one-page record: name, owner, purpose, tier, data it touches, the human check, how to pause it, and the date of the next look. Update it when something surprises you, so the tiers become a learning system. That is scaled learning applied to risk.

Start this month

  1. Write the three paragraphs. Use the definitions above as a draft and edit them until a practitioner on your team can place a use without calling you.
  2. Inventory the uses you can see this week, including the unofficial ones. Place each one. Where the design and the tier disagree, fix the design or the placement. Do not leave a disagreement undocumented.
  3. For one medium or high use, walk the questions with the owner and write the one-page record. You will learn which questions your teams cannot yet answer. That list is the real backlog.
  4. Name who reviews medium uses quickly, and who must see a high use. A review that has no named people will not happen on a busy week.
  5. Put a monthly half hour on the calendar for surprises, near misses and tier moves. Capture one lesson each time.

The longer playbook for roles, monitoring and incident response is The AI Governance Playbook, with the data side in Data Governance in the Age of AI. Teams that want to practice the judgment on their own uses can take Agentic AI for Transformation Teams as a facilitated session, or ask us to run a working review as part of enterprise workshops. Bring the inventory. The tier paragraphs are only useful once they have met a real use.
