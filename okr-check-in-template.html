An OKR check-in template teams will actually use

A weekly OKR check-in is a short conversation about confidence, what changed, what is blocked and what to decide. This template keeps those four things on the page and the status story off it.

By Jonathan Davis · Published November 16, 2026 · 13 min read

Teams drop OKRs mid-quarter when the weekly check-in becomes a status meeting: owners narrate activity, nobody decides, and the key result is typed in at the end because the ritual requires it. The check-in should be a short look at whether the outcome is still moving, and a decision while there is still time.

This guide is the template we use for that conversation, and the small set of rules that keep it honest. It is the weekly piece of the rhythm in our AI-driven OKRs guide and in the book AI-Driven OKRs.

What the check-in is for

The check-in is not a report to a manager. It is a conversation among the people who own the objectives. Its job is to surface, early, the key result that is quietly dying, the dependency nobody has named, and the objective that no longer matches the facts. If the meeting can be replaced by an email of completed tasks, it is the wrong meeting.

Twenty to thirty minutes is enough for one team when people arrive prepared. A cross-team check-in can run longer, and it still talks about decisions and dependencies, not a tour of tasks.

One owner speaks for each key result: the person who can explain the signal and ask for a decision. Send spectators the note afterward.

The template

Copy this into the place your team already works. One block per key result. Do not add fields for decoration.

  1. Objective and key result. The words you agreed at the start of the cycle, plus the owner. If you cannot say them without opening a slide, they are too vague to check.
  2. Signal this week. The latest observation: the number, the customer behavior or the qualitative evidence you said you would watch. One or two lines. If you have no signal, write “no signal” and say why. Silence is a finding.
  3. Confidence. On track, at risk or off track. The owner chooses, in words, and adds one sentence on why. Confidence is a judgment about the path to the result, not a recap of how busy the team was.
  4. What changed. Since the last check-in, what moved the signal, or what failed to. Include a change in the world, not only a change in your activity.
  5. Blocker or dependency. The single thing outside the owner's control that most threatens the result. If there are three, name the one that matters this week.
  6. Decision or ask. What you need from this room: a change in scope, a conversation with another team, a drop, a raise in attention, or nothing. “Nothing” is allowed. A fake ask is not.
  7. Lesson, if there is one. A surprise worth keeping. Leave it blank when the week was ordinary. An empty lesson field is healthier than a forced insight.

If your tool requires a score, put the confidence word beside it so the score does not become the conversation.

How to prepare so the meeting is a conversation

Owners fill their blocks before the meeting, the day before if you can. The meeting is for listening and deciding, not for watching someone remember what happened. An assistant or agent can draft the signal line from the system that holds the number, and it can remind an owner whose block is empty. It should not choose the confidence or the ask. Those are judgments. The OKR guide separates the same line: agents prepare, people decide.

Read the blocks beforehand and mark the ones that need the room. On track, with no blocker and no ask, gets a short confirmation. Off track, or carrying an ask, gets the time. Equal airtime is how the meeting becomes a recital.

If a signal has no trustworthy source, say so and fix the source. Do not let an agent update a bad number on a perfect schedule. When the number and the story disagree, you have a question. The guide to AI-driven balanced scorecards helps when the key result sits inside a wider set of measures.

How to run the twenty minutes

  1. Open with the marked blocks. Owner states confidence, the change and the ask. Two minutes each, then questions.
  2. Decide the ask in the room or name the person and the day it will be decided. An ask that leaves without a next step will return next week in the same words.
  3. Look across, not only down. After the marked blocks, ask whether two key results are fighting each other, or whether one team's blocker is another team's work. This is the part a status email cannot do.
  4. Touch the quiet ones. A key result that has been “on track” for weeks with no change in the signal deserves a question. Steady confidence with a frozen signal is often a key result nobody is really using.
  5. Close with the list of decisions and any lesson worth filing. Read them back. The note from the meeting is that list, plus the blocks. It is not a transcript.

Keep a facilitator who is not trying to defend their own key result at the same time, even if that person is a peer on a rotation. The job is to stop the story of activity and return to confidence, blocker and ask.

What to do with the notes

The blocks are a record of judgment over time. Read them at the mid-cycle look and at the end of the cycle. The pattern matters more than any single week: a key result that slid from on track to at risk and stayed there, an ask that was repeated and never answered, a lesson you wrote three times in different words.

At mid-cycle, use the notes to ask a harder question than the weekly meeting has time for. Is the objective still the right objective? A key result can be changed in the open when the facts change. Quietly ignoring it until the end of the quarter teaches the team that the check-in is decorative.

At the end of the cycle the notes feed the retrospective. An agent can assemble a draft. The team corrects it and writes what it learned about the work, the measures and the goals. The next quarter starts from that lesson. That is the bridge to retrospectives that actually change things, and it is why Continuous Conversations treats the check-in as a learning rhythm.

How this fails

  • Activity in the signal line. “Shipped the flow” is not a signal unless shipping was the outcome you agreed to watch. Ask what the customer or the business can now see.
  • Confidence as optimism. If at risk and off track are punished, every block will say on track until the last week. Leaders set this tone by thanking an early at risk more than a late surprise.
  • Too many key results in the room. If you cannot mark and talk through the risky ones in half an hour, you are checking in on a backlog, not on outcomes. Cut the set. Our book Outcome-Driven AI is about making that cut and keeping it.
  • The template as a form to complete. Filling every box is not the goal. A blank lesson and a real decision beat a complete form and no decision.
  • No link to a person. A key result without an owner has no one to state confidence. Do not check it in until it has one.

If the team is setting OKRs for the first time, start with the practical guide and then bring this template to week two, not week twelve.
